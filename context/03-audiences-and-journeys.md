# 03 — Audiences, motivations and journeys

This is the most important file for copy and UX. The website is not selling a commodity; it is asking a person to disclose a private wound to a stranger. Understanding who they are and what they fear matters more than the layout.

---

## 1. The emotional truth of the category

People arrive at this business in one of five states:

1. **Longing** — "I have thought about this person my whole life and I finally have the means to look."
2. **Guilt** — "I left / I gave them up / I didn't look sooner."
3. **Grief** — "I need to know before they die, or before I die."
4. **Fear** — "What if they reject me? What if they are dead? What if my family finds out?"
5. **Suspicion** — "Is this real, or am I about to be scammed again?"

The copy must speak to the person, not to "users". It must be warm without being saccharine, and confident without promising a miracle.

## 2. Audience segments

### A. Adult children searching for a biological parent
**Who:** 25–60, often raised by a single mother or adoptive family; may have a name, an old photo, a town, or almost nothing. Many are women; many are from the provinces or the diaspora.
**Trigger:** a birthday, becoming a parent, a death, a DNA test, a document, a comment from a relative.
**What they want:** identity, medical history, a face, an explanation, a relationship — or simply closure.
**What they fear:** rejection, disrupting the parent's current family, discovering abuse or indifference, wasting money.
**What they say:** "Solo quiero saber si está vivo." "No quiero molestar, solo conocerlo." "Mi mamá nunca me quiso decir nada."
**Decision criteria:** discretion, a real human on the phone, proof that the agency has done this before, a clear first step, a price they can afford.

### B. Parents searching for a child
**Who:** parents who lost contact through separation, migration, conflict, adoption, abduction, or a child who ran away; sometimes elderly parents with limited digital literacy.
**Trigger:** illness, old age, a death in the family, a family event, a persistent memory.
**What they want:** to see the child, to know they are alive, to ask forgiveness, to restore a bond.
**What they fear:** rejection, learning the child died, being judged as a bad parent, public exposure.
**Copy implication:** do not blame the parent. Use "reencuentro", not "abandono".

### C. Siblings and extended family
**Who:** brothers/sisters separated by divorce, migration, adoption, conflict, or a parent's disappearance; grandchildren looking for a family branch.
**Trigger:** a death, an inheritance, a family reunion, a photo, a DNA match.
**What they want:** family completeness, a shared history, descendants who know where they come from.
**What they say:** "Somos nueve hermanos y solo conozco a tres." "Quiero que mis hijos sepan de dónde vienen."
**Copy implication:** "la familia completa" is a powerful phrase in their own testimonials.

### D. Families of missing persons
**Who:** families of someone who disappeared — migration, crime, conflict, mental health, trafficking, accident, or simply leaving home.
**Trigger:** an anniversary, a sighting, a police lead, a new year without the person.
**What they want:** news, a body, closure, a reunion.
**What they fear:** being exploited, false hope, the cost of a search that may never end.
**Copy implication:** this is the most fragile segment. Never promise a happy ending. Offer honesty and accompaniment.

### E. The Peruvian diaspora and cross-border families
**Who:** Peruvians in Chile, Argentina, the US, Spain, Italy, Canada; foreigners with Peruvian roots; families split across borders.
**Trigger:** a relative's illness, a planned trip, retirement, a child asking about their roots.
**What they want:** someone on the ground in Peru who can act while they are abroad.
**Language:** Spanish, English, Italian. Time zones matter.
**Copy implication:** foreground "we work in Peru and with collaborators abroad", WhatsApp, and international payment.

### F. Family lawyers, notaries and public defenders
**Who:** lawyers handling child support, inheritance, paternity, guardianship, or notification; notaries who need an address.
**Trigger:** a case that needs a person located.
**What they want:** speed, a written report, legal admissibility, a reliable partner, an invoice.
**Copy implication:** a separate B2B path with "para abogados" language, response times, and documentation.

### G. Spouses/partners suspecting infidelity
**Who:** adults in a relationship where trust has broken; often women, but not only.
**Trigger:** a change in routine, a suspicion, a friend's warning, a pre-divorce decision.
**What they want:** the truth and evidence, fast and discreet.
**What they fear:** being judged, being discovered, wasting money, illegal evidence.
**Copy implication:** non-judgmental, evidence-focused, absolute discretion, "lawful evidence".

### H. Fraud victims
**Who:** individuals and small businesses who lost money to a scam, fake investment, fake purchase, or impersonation.
**Trigger:** the loss, a police report, a lawyer's request.
**What they want:** the fraudster identified and located; money recovered if possible.
**What they fear:** another scam; being told it is hopeless.
**Copy implication:** transparent process, realistic expectations, coordination with authorities.

### I. SMEs and corporate clients
**Who:** owners, managers, HR, compliance, loss prevention.
**Trigger:** inventory shrinkage, a tip-off, a suspicious payment, a data leak, an impersonation.
**What they want:** a discreet internal investigation, evidence, dismissal or prosecution, reputation protection.
**Copy implication:** confidentiality, legality, chain of custody, "no damage to your operation".

### J. People searching for an ex-partner, friend or acquaintance
**Who:** adults trying to reconnect with a first love, a childhood friend, a schoolmate, a benefactor.
**Trigger:** nostalgia, a life transition, a reunion.
**What they want:** a contact, a conversation, closure.
**Copy implication:** include this in the family-search umbrella without equating it to "missing persons".

## 3. Composite personas (illustrative, not real clients)

> These are constructed from the public case stories and testimonials. They are **not** real individuals and must not be presented as such.

### "María, 38 — Lima → Iquitos"
She was raised by her grandmother. Her mother always said her father "went to the jungle". She has a first name, a rough age, and a 30-year-old photo. She is scared of being rejected but more scared of never knowing. She uses WhatsApp daily, Facebook occasionally, and has never hired a professional service. She will judge the agency by how quickly and kindly someone answers her first message.

**What she needs on the site:** a confidential form, a human WhatsApp, an explanation of what happens next, a price range or "consultation is free", and stories of people like her.

### "Jorge, 45 — Santiago, Chile"
His Peruvian mother left when he was a child. He has her name and a story about Arequipa. He speaks Spanish, works long hours, and can only call after 8 pm. He is worried about being scammed from abroad and wants to pay securely.

**What he needs:** international service clarity, time-zone-friendly contact, a video call option, a transparent process, and proof of real cases.

### "Rosa, 62 — Cajamarca"
She is looking for a son who left at 17 and never came back. She is not comfortable with forms. Her grandson will help her send the WhatsApp message. She has no photos of him as an adult.

**What she needs:** large type, simple language, a phone number that a human answers, and patience.

### "Estudio Vargas — Lima"
A law firm needs the current address of a father who owes 8 years of child support. They need a report they can attach to a filing, a timeline, and an invoice.

**What they need:** a B2B page, documented deliverables, legal language, and a direct line.

### "Daniel, 34 — Miraflores"
He suspects his partner is unfaithful and wants photos for a divorce. He is ashamed to be asking and wants absolute discretion.

**What he needs:** a calm, non-judgmental page, a clear statement of lawful methods, and a private channel.

### "Andrea, 41 — owner of a distribution company"
Inventory is short by 12% and she suspects a warehouse supervisor. She cannot afford a public scandal.

**What they need:** a corporate investigation page, confidentiality, a clear methodology, and a "request a confidential consultation" CTA.

## 4. Jobs to be done

| When… | I want to… | So I can… |
|---|---|---|
| I have a name and almost nothing else | know whether a search is even possible | decide whether to start |
| I am afraid of being scammed | see real people, a real address, real cases | trust enough to send my story |
| I don't want my family to know | understand how confidentiality works | protect my privacy |
| The person may not want to see me | know how you handle a refusal | avoid causing harm |
| I live abroad | know you can act in Peru without me | delegate the search |
| I need evidence for court | know what I will receive and whether it is admissible | build my case |
| I am a lawyer | have a reliable partner and a written report | serve my client |
| I am a company | investigate without damaging operations | protect the business |
| I cannot afford a big fee | understand the cost before I share my story | decide without wasting time |

## 5. The decision journey

### Stage 0 — Latent need
The person has thought about the search for years but has not acted. They may not know such a service exists, or assume it is only for the rich, or fear it is a scam.

**Content that helps:** SEO pages for "cómo buscar a mi padre biológico", "encontrar a un familiar desaparecido", real reunion stories, short TikTok videos.

### Stage 1 — Trigger and search
A birthday, a death, a DNA test, a document, a family conversation. They search Google or Facebook: "buscar personas Perú", "encontrar a mi papá", "agencia de búsqueda de personas", "buscar familiares en Perú".

**What they find today:** the website (generic title, confusing hero), the Facebook page (active, emotional, but not a structured path), TikTok (case videos), directories.

**What they need:** a clear, credible landing page that says "yes, this is what we do" in the first five seconds.

### Stage 2 — Evaluation
They compare the agency with free Facebook groups, other "busca personas" sites, lawyers, and the police. They look for:
- Is this a real organisation with an address and a history?
- Are the cases real?
- Is it legal?
- Will my data be safe?
- How much will it cost?
- Will they treat me with respect?

**Current trust leaks:** template testimonials, fake case cards, lorem ipsum, inconsistent numbers, no privacy policy, no team bios, a broken logo.

### Stage 3 — First contact
Usually WhatsApp. Sometimes the web form, Facebook Messenger, or a phone call. They may write a long, emotional story at midnight.

**What they need:**
- A reply that acknowledges the emotion, not just the logistics.
- A short, human intake: name, country, who they are looking for, what they know, what they can share.
- A clear statement of confidentiality and next steps.
- No pressure to pay immediately.

### Stage 4 — Intake and feasibility
The agency reviews the information, may ask for documents/photos, and gives an assessment and quote.

**What they need:** honesty about what is possible, a realistic timeframe, a written scope, and a price.

### Stage 5 — Investigation
Weeks or months of work. The client is anxious and may need updates.

**What they need:** a named contact, agreed update cadence, and clarity about what "no news yet" means.

### Stage 6 — Location and approach
The person is found. The agency must decide how to approach them, respecting their wishes and the client's safety.

**What they need:** guidance, emotional preparation, and a plan for the first contact.

### Stage 7 — Reunion
Phone, video, or in person. Sometimes the person has died; sometimes they refuse; sometimes the family is complete.

**What they need:** support, not just a phone number. This is where the business's social-worker/psychologist layer matters.

### Stage 8 — After
Testimonial, public story, referral, or silence. Some clients want to tell the world; others want to disappear.

**What they need:** privacy by default, and an easy way to say no to publicity.

The live site embeds a JotForm (ID `50398505051654`). Fields:

| Field | Required | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nombre completo (Nombres / Apellidos) | Yes | |
| Nacionalidad | No | |
| Número de teléfono | Yes | |
| Email | Yes | |
| Datos de la persona que busca (Nombre / Apellido) | Yes | |
| Narre la historia del caso | Yes | Free text |
| Fotos o documentos | No | File upload |
| Submit | — | "Enviar" |

**What is missing from the form:**
- Country/city of residence and time zone.
- Relationship to the person sought.
- What is already known (date/place of birth, last known address, documents).
- Whether the person sought knows they are being looked for / consent constraints.
- Preferred language.
- How they prefer to be contacted and when.
- A clear privacy consent checkbox and link to a privacy policy.
- A "this is urgent / safety risk" flag.
- A budget range or "how did you hear about us".

**What the form does well:** it asks for the story in the client's own words and allows documents/photos. That is the right instinct.

## 7. Case lifecycle (operational model)

```
MESSAGE
  ↓
TRIAGE (human, confidential)
  ↓
INTAKE (form + call + documents)
  ↓
FEASIBILITY (databases, preliminary checks)
  ↓
PROPOSAL (scope, price, timeline, confidentiality agreement)
  ↓
INVESTIGATION (desk + field + network + legal)
  ↓
VERIFICATION (identity confirmation; DNA/forensics if needed)
  ↓
APPROACH (to the person sought, with consent/sensitivity)
  ↓
REUNION (call/video/in person; emotional + legal support)
  ↓
CLOSURE (report, follow-up, testimonial if consented)
```

**Unknown operational details:** who does triage, response time, case management system, client updates, payment schedule, refunds, success definition, reporting format.

## 8. Objections and how to answer them (content requirements)

| Objection | What the person is really saying | What the site must show |
|---|---|---|
| "¿Es real o es una estafa?" | I have been hurt before. | Real names, address, page history, case stories, reviews, a human voice, no pressure. |
| "¿Es legal?" | I don't want to get in trouble. | Lawful methods, judicial authorisation where needed, privacy policy, ethical stance. |
| "¿Y si la persona no quiere verme?" | I don't want to hurt anyone. | A clear protocol: we ask, we don't force; the person's consent matters. |
| "¿Cuánto cuesta?" | I need to know if I can afford hope. | A consultation, a range, or a clear "we will tell you after we understand the case". |
| "¿Cuánto tarda?" | I am afraid of waiting forever. | Honest ranges, update cadence, what happens if nothing is found. |
| "¿Mis datos están seguros?" | I am ashamed / scared. | Confidentiality, who sees the file, retention, deletion, no public posting without consent. |
| "¿Y si ya murió?" | I need to prepare for the worst. | Compassionate language; the possibility of confirming a death with dignity. |
| "¿Puedo hacerlo desde otro país?" | I can't travel. | International service, WhatsApp, video calls, secure payment. |
| "¿Hablo inglés/italiano?" | I need to understand. | Real language options, not just a claim. |
| "¿Por qué no lo hago gratis en Facebook?" | I don't see the value. | The difference between a post and an investigation: active search, legal channels, discretion, a human intermediary. |

## 9. Ethical and sensitive-content guidelines for the new site

These are not legal advice; they are content principles drawn from the business's own risks.

1. **Consent before publicity.** Do not publish a client's name, face, or story without documented consent. The current site does; the new one should not repeat that by default.
2. **Do not expose the located person.** A reunion story may require masking surnames, faces, locations, and identifying details.
3. **Minors:** extra care. Do not publish images or identifying details of children.
4. **No false hope.** Use "buscamos", "intentamos", "evaluamos la viabilidad", not "garantizado".
5. **No illegal methods.** Never imply access to private databases, phone tapping, hacking, or surveillance that violates privacy.
6. **The person sought has rights.** State that contact will be respectful and consensual.
7. **Grief-aware language.** If the person is deceased, the site should have a compassionate path, not a dead end.
8. **Scam-awareness.** The category is full of fraud. Publish how to verify the agency, how payments work, and what they will never ask for.
9. **Faith language is authentic but not exclusionary.** Many testimonials invoke God; the brand can be warm and hopeful without requiring a religious identity.
10. **Do not use "terapias" unless the business actually provides therapy.** The user's brief used the word "therapies"; the business describes investigation and accompaniment, not clinical therapy. If counselling is offered, name the professional and the scope.

## 11. What the audience needs to see, in order

1. **"This is for someone like me."** A hero that names the situation (father, mother, sibling, child, missing person) — not an abstract "investigations".
2. **"This is real and safe."** Faces, names, roles, address, years, languages, reviews.
3. **"I understand what will happen."** A 3–5 step process, including the first conversation and confidentiality.
4. **"I can afford to ask."** A consultation, a range, or a clear "no obligation".
5. **"They have done this before."** Consented case stories with real outcomes, including difficult ones.
6. **"I can start right now."** WhatsApp and a short form, both mobile-first.
7. **"They will not expose me."** A plain-language privacy and ethics promise.
